Experiment 1: which predicted-plausibility metrics can be trusted?
Damage-ladder calibration on phiX174-like phage genomes (Microviridae), with a small Evo 2 check  ·  29 October 2026  ·  capstone
1. Aim
Predicted biological plausibility means software predicts that a genome has what a phiX174-like phage needs: genes, protein families, protein shapes, gene order. These are predictions, not proof that the genome works. Before using them to judge generated genomes, we need to know which ones actually notice damage. The plan is to take real phage genomes, damage them in known ways, and ask each metric four questions:
Sensitivity: does the score get worse as the damage grows?
False alarms: does it stay unchanged when the change is harmless?
Independence: does it notice damage that k-mer statistics (short DNA word counts) cannot see?
Robustness: do real genomes pass it, and do different tools agree?
Then real Evo 2 output is scored on the same scale. No viability claim is made anywhere. The calibration needs no GPU and works for any phage family, so it is the main result; the Evo 2 part is a small add-on.

3. Data
Natural set: complete Microviridae genomes of 4 to 6.5 kb from NCBI, including phiX174 relatives. Cluster at 95% nucleotide identity and keep one genome per cluster, so near-copies are not counted many times. Put all genomes in the same orientation and start (by matching the replication gene).
Target: at least 90 clusters, split 30 / 60. The 30 calibration genomes define the natural range, the k-mer table for D8, and the reference gene families for M6, M8, M9. The 60 test genomes are scored. I did not count how many genomes exist; one 2012 study alone assembled 81 new complete Microviridae genomes, so hundreds is plausible. With only 60 clusters in total (20 / 40) the AUROC intervals widen to about ±0.10.
Why 60 test genomes: AUROC (0.5 = cannot tell apart, 1.0 = always can) for a true value of 0.85 has a 95% interval of about ±0.07 with 60 genomes per group, and ±0.10 with 30 (Hanley-McNeil formula, assumes independent genomes).

6. The Evo 2 check
Take the first 1,000 letters (G1) or 300 letters (G2) of 40 test genomes and let Evo 2 7B run through EvSpark (pretrained, temperature 1.0, top-k 4, the published default; EvSpark is a speed-up whose output the authors report has the same statistics as normal Evo 2) continue to the natural genome&apos;s length, so each output has a natural partner. That is 80 sequences of about 4.4 to 5.1 kb. No fine-tuning: King et al. fine-tuned on about 15,000 genomes, which does not fit a $30 budget, and their pretrained models did not recall phiX174 at any prompt length tested, so many outputs may look nothing like phiX174. That is fine for this purpose. Score them with the full panel and report two things: the distribution of each metric against the natural range, and the equivalent damage, the severity of each damage type whose metric profile is closest to the output.
7. Analysis and decision rules (fixed in advance; thresholds are my suggestions)
Sensitivity: AUROC of damaged vs natural test genomes at the middle severity, with a 95% bootstrap interval (resampling genomes). Dose response: Spearman correlation between severity and score. Pass: AUROC at least 0.85 with the lower bound at least 0.75, for each damage the metric was predicted to catch (●).
False alarms: AUROC for D1 and D10 must lie between 0.40 and 0.60. Otherwise the metric is fragile.
Independence: a metric claimed to measure gene or protein plausibility must separate D7 or D8 (where predicted ●). A metric that scores D8 as natural is texture-level only.
Natural pass rate: define &quot;pass&quot; as inside the calibration set&apos;s 5th to 95th percentile. At least 90% of natural test genomes should pass. Standard gene finders miss genes in natural phiX174 (King et al.), so M4 and M5 may fail this; that would be a finding, and the reason M6 exists.
Agreement: Spearman correlation between M4 and M5, and among M11 to M13, across genomes.
Verdict per metric: Keep (all four pass), Keep with caveat (say which fails), or Drop for validity. Report results as a metric by damage grid of AUROCs, not p-values; 15 metrics by 10 damages would make single p-values misleading.
8. Budget on Modal ($30 free credit) and run order
Prices differ between sources (A100 80GB: about $2.50/h vs $3.72/h; H100 about $3.95/h; T4 $0.59, A10 $1.10), so check Modal&apos;s page. Speed is the main unknown. NVIDIA&apos;s Evo 2 benchmarking page lists minimum throughputs of 40 to 52 letters/s for plain 7B on one GPU. EvSpark&apos;s authors report about 2.0 to 2.4 times faster on E. coli at long context, but say the gain depends on the region and is smallest on bacterial coding DNA, and I have no figure for phage. I assume 1.5 to 2.5 times, so 60 to 120 letters/s.

9. If phiX174 does not work: other cheap options

10. To verify before running
How many natural Microviridae clusters remain after 95% deduplication (not counted).
That Pharokka, Phold and CheckV run on 5 kb circular single-stranded phage records, and how they treat the circular start.
Whether the pretrained Evo 2 7B produces phiX174-like output from a 300 or 1,000 letter prompt (King et al. report it did not recall phiX174).
Real Evo 2 speed and cost on Modal (stage 4), and current Modal prices.
EvSpark has never been run by me. Its code needs a prompt of at least 64 letters (128 in its candidate interface), loads the 7B model and a published drafter checkpoint, and accepts temperature and top-k. Step 4 must confirm all of this. If it will not install or its lossless check fails, fall back to plain Evo 2 at the slower 40 to 52 letters/s (about $7 to 10 for step 5).
